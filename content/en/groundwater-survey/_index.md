## The short version

Water moves through the ground differently from solid rock, dry soil or clay, and that changes how easily electricity passes through it. A resistivity survey measures that along a line on your property and produces an image of the subsurface. A geologist then reads the image together with what's known about the local geology and recommends where drilling looks most promising.

## What happens on your property

1. **Before the visit:** Phase 1 identifies where the survey line(s) should go.
2. **On site:** a crew lays out a cable with metal stakes (electrodes) along a straight line, often several hundred feet long. A small current is passed through the ground and the response is recorded. It's non-invasive: no digging or drilling, and the ground is left as it was found.
3. **After the visit:** the data is processed into cross-section images and interpreted by independent partner geologists.
4. **Report:** you receive the images, the recommended drilling location(s), and the stated limitations.

## What the results can and can't tell you

Resistivity is an **indirect** measurement. Clay, moisture, rock type, fractures and mineral content all affect it. That's why a color on the image is never treated as proof of water on its own.

What the survey can do is show where conditions look more or less favorable along the line, so your driller starts from evidence rather than a guess. Depth and detail depend on the setup and the ground, so the report says what the data supports at your site.

## Is a survey right for your property?

Most rural residential and farm properties are suitable. A survey needs a fairly clear, straight path for the line, so dense brush may need clearing (quoted in advance), and nearby buried metal, pipes or power lines can affect readings. Phase 1 checks these things before anyone visits.
