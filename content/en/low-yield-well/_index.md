## First, rule out the equipment

Not every weak well needs replacing. Before paying for a new well, ask a licensed well or pump contractor to check:

- **The pump and pressure system**: a failing pump, pressure switch or tank can look like a water shortage.
- **The well itself**: clogged screens, mineral buildup or a damaged casing can choke off flow.
- **Usage and season**: demand that grew (new household members, irrigation, livestock) or a dry season can push a marginal well past its limit.

If the problem is the well's equipment, a new location won't help. We'll ask about this when you contact us, and point you to the right kind of contractor if a study isn't what you need.

## When a new well is the right call

If the existing well simply doesn't reach enough water, the next well shouldn't be drilled by guesswork. That's where a study helps:

- **Phase 1 (remote)** looks at the geology, terrain and nearby well records to find the more promising parts of your property, and whether an on-site survey is worth doing.
- **Phase 2 (on site)** collects electrical resistivity data along chosen line(s), so partner geologists can look for features such as fracture zones that often carry water in crystalline rock.

You get a report with recommended drilling location(s) to discuss with your driller. Information about your current well, such as its depth, yield and when it was drilled, is useful context, so include what you know when you [request a review](/request/).
